Where it started

Pregnancy is overwhelming, and existing apps weren't helping

There are dozens of pregnancy apps, and almost all of them do one or two things reasonably well and nothing else: the week-by-week tracker, the symptom logger, the community forum. What you don't get is something that helps a pregnant woman manage the full reality of her day:

Her nutritionHer mental stateHer appointmentsHer to-do listHer sleep
The insight

The women we spoke to weren't looking for another article about folic acid.

They wanted something that felt like it understood their situation and helped them get through the day.

The brief

Seven features. One connected product.

An AI-powered wellness app that brings seven things into one coherent experience. My job was to make it feel like a single, connected product, not a collection of features bolted together.

Stage 4 · Visual direction

Not pastel. Not clinical. A third way.

Pregnancy apps almost universally go one of two ways: pastel cartoons, which feel designed for the baby, or clinical white, which feels like a hospital app. We went a third way: a deep, dark base with lavender and rose pink as warm, feminine accents.

Rejected: PastelFeels designed for babies, not mothers
Rejected: ClinicalCold and impersonal for a deeply personal experience
Chosen: Warm darkComfortable at 3am, distinctly feminine, grown-up

Why dark: pregnancy disrupts sleep, and a lot of app usage happens at night. A bright white screen when you can't sleep is the last thing you want.

Design principles

Every screen assumes she might be tired, nauseous or anxious

Or dealing with something urgent. So nothing on screen should require reading a paragraph.

  1. 1
    Generous touch targetsQuick actions are big tiles, easy to hit when she is exhausted or in a hurry.
  2. 2
    Short textOne line per card: who, when and how, then stop.
  3. 3
    Clear hierarchyOne obvious next step on each card: Join call, Book, Start session.
  4. 4
    AI woven in quietlyMeal plans, music curation and task suggestions show up as help, not as features to learn.

    What I learned

    What I'd carry forward

    01

    Design for the person, not the condition.

    Pregnant women are adults managing complex lives. The design language needed to reflect that: sophisticated, warm and clear, not cute or patronising.

    02

    Holistic means designed holistically, not just listed holistically.

    It's easy to say "we cover nutrition, sleep, mental health and tasks." Making those feel like one coherent experience instead of separate tabs is the actual design work.

    03

    Tired users need simple screens.

    Short text. Big targets. Clear hierarchy. The person using this at 3am can't be expected to read a tooltip or figure out a novel interaction pattern.

    04

    The AI should be invisible until it's needed.

    The value of AI personalisation isn't the label "AI-powered". It's the moment a user opens the app and thinks "how did it know I needed this?"

    Reflection

    Designing for a moving target

    Her needs in week 8 are completely different from week 32: physically, emotionally and informationally.

    What it taught me

    The system had to be flexible enough to personalise across the entire journey, not just at onboarding.

    And feature completeness is not the same as a good product. Every app we looked at had features. None of them had coherence.

    If I did it again

    I'd push harder on the AI personalisation, earlier.

    Meal planning and music curation needed more definition around how the system learns and adapts week by week. That's what makes MOMents genuinely different, and it deserved more design attention than it got in the initial prototype.

    Pregnancy isn't a problem to be solved. It's an experience to be supported.

    That's the difference between a feature set and a product that actually helps.
