A tool that listens, so doctors don't have to type.

An AI consultation scribe for Capgemini's internal health clinic. It records the visit once, then hands it back two ways: a clinical record the doctor signs, and a plain-language app for the patient.

The short version

Record the visit once. Hand it back two ways.

The problem

In a ten-minute consultation, the doctor spent half of it typing instead of listening. The patient left remembering maybe half of what was said.

What I designed

A consultation screen that only listens, a summary the doctor reviews and signs, and a plain-language app for the patient. Five screens, one source of truth.

What I learned

In healthcare, a tool earns trust before it gets to help. One wrong line in a summary, and a doctor stops believing all of it.

The brief

In a ten-minute consultation, five minutes went to the keyboard.

Capgemini ran an internal health clinic, and the brief came out of one observation.

The brief

Give doctors back the half of the appointment they lose to a keyboard, for the doctor and for the patient on the other side of the desk.

Who it was for

Same data, three very different people.

That gave me three problems to design for, not one.

The doctor

Put a screen in front of them mid-consultation and they'll look at it. They can't help it.

What they neededA screen that earns its place in the room without pulling their eyes off the patient.

The patient

They leave having absorbed half of what was said, lose the paper script and forget the dose.

What they neededThe visit handed back in plain language, with medication reminders that set themselves.

The next specialist

They meet the patient cold and rebuild the history from memory, usually getting bits of it wrong.

What they neededThe whole record in front of them, without a phone call to the GP's office.

Principles

Three rules I designed against.

1

The consultation screen has to stay invisible.

The consultation is between the doctor and the patient. The app is there to record it, not to join in.

2

The patient app can't read like a medical record.

The patient gets the visit back in plain language, written with the clinical team.

3

Trust is the feature.

A doctor won't sign their name to something they don't believe.

The system

Five screens, one source of truth.

The same record powers everything. It's captured passively in the room, reviewed by the doctor, then shown two ways: the clinical version for anyone treating the patient, and a plain-language version for the patient.     In the room

    Most of the work was deciding what to take out.

    The first version

    Put the transcript front and centre, with buttons on the AI suggestions and playback controls. Comprehensive, and exactly the kind of thing a doctor minimises and never reopens.

    So we cut
    • The flag-a-line control
    • The accept-suggestion button
    • Playback controls
    • Anything else that asked for a tap

    Every removal made it better.

    The rule we landed on

    If the doctor has to touch this screen mid-consultation, something has gone wrong.

    Principle 1 · The screen stays invisible

    The record

    One wrong line, and a doctor stops believing the whole summary.

    Trust took the longest. Doctors reviewing the auto-generated summary would catch a single wrong line and stop believing the whole thing.

    What came out of it
    • Confidence scoring
    • Obvious edit affordances
    • Section-by-section review

    For the patient

    After the visit, the app hands it back.

    Once the doctor finalises the summary, the patient app updates on its own. No manual entry: everything flows from the same recording session.

    • Plain-language visit summary, not clinical jargon
    • Medication reminders pre-set from prescription timings
    • Full history for any authorised doctor
    • Lab results and follow-up notifications
    • Prescription as a QR code for the pharmacy

    Reminders that set themselves.

    Nobody configures a reminder schedule after a GP appointment. So the prescription's timings become the reminders, and a dose change updates them on its own.

    No paper script to lose. No test left unexplained.

    The prescription lives in the app as a QR code any pharmacy can scan, and each pending test says, in plain words, why it was ordered.

    Validation

    It went back to the people it was for.

    Doctors tested the prototypes. The sharpest signal was the summary-trust problem: one error collapsing confidence in the whole record. The review flow exists because of how hard they pushed on it.

    Reflection

    What I'd keep, and what I'd change.

    Keep

    The passive consultation screen.

    Every push to make it interactive got resisted, and that restraint is what made it work. The consultation is between the doctor and the patient; the app's job is to record it, not join in.

    Change

    Design consent first.

    Consent got squeezed to the end of the timeline. In healthcare it belongs at the start, not as a finishing touch. Next time I'd design the "who sees what" flow first.

    Reflection

    "Positive feedback" isn't a measurement.

    I didn't set success metrics going in. Next time I'd agree on what good looks like before building, and track these three.

    Metric 01Time to finalise a recordFrom the end of the session to a signed summary.
    Metric 02Corrections per generated summaryHow much of the draft the doctor has to fix.
    Metric 03Patients opening the app within 24 hours of a visitWhether the hand-back actually reaches them.

    What I learned

    Four things this project taught me.

    01

    Designing for two users means accepting they want opposite things.

    The doctor wants less to look at; the patient wants more explained. Same data, opposite jobs. The trap would have been forcing them into one interface.

    02

    In healthcare, a tool earns trust before it gets to help.

    One wrong code in a generated summary and a doctor is done with the whole system. Confidence scoring and the review state weren't polish. They were the price of being used at all.

    03

    Plain language is more work, not less.

    "Hypertension, controlled" is two words. Saying it so a patient actually understands takes someone who knows both the medicine and the person reading it.

    04

    A screen that asks for nothing is still doing its job.

    The consultation interface needed zero interaction during the session. Being present without demanding attention was the entire point.

    The rule, one more time

    The consultation is between the doctor and the patient. The app's job is to record it, not join in.
