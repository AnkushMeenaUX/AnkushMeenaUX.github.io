Background

They knew their equipment inside out. The tool didn't match how they worked.

Through Capgemini, I was placed at Baker Hughes as a UX designer on the internal asset management platform, where field engineers register new equipment, track health and maintenance history, and raise service requests.

My brief

Requirements came from the stakeholder side, so what the system had to capture was already decided. My job was to figure out how a real person, probably under time pressure on an offshore platform, could do this accurately without needing to think about the tool.

What's at stake

A miscategorised asset or a missed commissioning date can mean a compliance failure, or a missed service on critical machinery.

Turbines, compressors, drilling tools and sensors, across offshore platforms and onshore facilities globally.

The problem

The forms were technically correct. They just didn't work.

Mandatory and optional fields looked identical. No progress indicator, no grouping. Engineers abandoned halfway through, or submitted with gaps on serial numbers and commissioning dates: required for compliance, but not visually distinct from anything else.

The solution

Five screens, one consistent sidebar.

Each screen has one job. The sidebar stays the same on all of them, so engineers never have to re-learn where they are. This is the working prototype: click through it, or hover a note to find it on the screen.

    Handoff

    A good prototype that doesn't survive handoff is just a good prototype.

    For every screen I delivered an annotated spec alongside the Figma prototype, and ran handoff sessions for the complex forms: not to explain the design, but to answer the questions that would otherwise come back as bugs two weeks into build.

    ~20interaction rules between the maintenance request form and the confirmation screen, each one documented.

    Reflection

    What I'd do differently, and what I wouldn't.

    Change

    Agree on success measures before designing.

    The feedback on the new flow was positive. With measures agreed up front, I could show exactly how much it moved.

    Keep

    Time spent on handoff documentation.

    It felt slow, adding annotation layers and writing spec notes. But it's what kept the build from diverging.

    Contextual error messages.

    "Required" on every field would have been faster. But engineers who know their domain deeply need the system to be honest about what it needs, and why.

    What I learned

    Four things I carry from this project.

    01

    Requirements come from the system. The design has to come from the user.

    Every requirement was accurate from a data perspective. The work was translating it into a flow that matched how a field engineer actually thinks during registration.

    02

    The confirmation screen is part of the form, not an afterthought.

    The form ends when the engineer is confident something happened, not when they click submit. In high-stakes workflows, a reference and a clear timeline are the last part of the design.

    03

    Annotated handoff is the last mile of interaction design.

    Developers building from well-documented specs don't have to make design decisions. Developers building from flat screens do. The difference shows up in production, not in review.

    04

    Inline validation only works if the messages are specific.

    "This field is required" tells an engineer nothing new. "Required for Class A compliance tracking" tells them why it matters, and makes it harder to skip.

    The thread through all of it

    Engineers who know their domain deeply need the system to be honest with them about what it needs, and why.
